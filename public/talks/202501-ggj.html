What you need for the game jam:

Things you might want to bring: a Wi-Fi-enabled computer and charging cables. You might want a pen and notebook to sketch out your ideas, or a digital tablet if you have one — sketchbooks, pencils, and a camera if you want to capture photos.

Music supplies: a mic, instruments, your own voice, software pre-installed on your computer. Of course you can install software the night of the jam, but if you have a particular area you want to focus on, it's totally fine to pre-install some software — you can do that tonight or tomorrow morning — things like photo editing software, music mixers, game engines, terminals.

Last but not least: some kind of version control. GitHub is the general standard for lots of things. Perforce is industry standard for video games, and Diversion is one I've just recently heard about through Johnny Cash's stream. Really, you only need one or two of these things — if you're not looking to specialize in anything, I think having just a computer and charging cables, or a pen and notebook, is enough.

I also want to call out that you don't have to make a digital game — you can make an analog game, a board game. In that case you don't necessarily need a computer either, unless you're looking to print something out. All that to say, games are things that people play, and that's not necessarily limited to digital things.

Art supplies:

You can use anything to make art. You can use physical things like pencils, paints, paper — whatever you want — as long as you can photograph it, you can use it. A lot of digital backgrounds in visual novels are actually photographs the artist has taken. For instance, if you wanted to go outside and take photos of Tokyo and then come back and use them for your game, that's totally fine.

Digital tools include a drawing tablet or a mouse — drawing with a mouse is really hard and messes up your wrists, so I don't necessarily recommend it, but if you want to go hard, go for it.

To give you a personal example: you don't really have to use any sophisticated art programs. A couple of years ago I did a game called Eek Bunny, about little bunnies with flowers growing on their backs. One of the diversifiers was putting all your assets in MS Paint, so I opened up a web-based MS Paint and made all these sprites. You don't necessarily have to make things sophisticated — just putting things together and drawing what you know is, I think, the key to getting your ideas out there.

I'm in the process of updating and polishing Eek Bunny — I tend to do things very high-effort as an artist, so I went from MS Paint to actual physical paints and did some work in watercolor for it. Hopefully I'll release that game sometime soon, but right now it's a back-burner side project, a cute thing I like working on from time to time. I used a stabilized iPhone camera and just took a picture of the watercolors, and that's what's going into the game. You can also use anything to make music — sound effects, Foley effects like crinkling paper or slapping to add sound to your games. Software overview — art, music, and 3D:

For art software: Photoshop, the Adobe suite, is something a lot of people use. Clip Studio is used a lot by comic artists and for pixel graphics/sprite work. An alternative to Photoshop is Affinity Photo, which has very similar features — if you're used to Photoshop but don't want a subscription, Affinity Photo might be a good fit. Krita is entirely free.

Procreate is what I'd consider the current standard when it comes to things like commissions — it's an iPad app that turns your iPad into a very powerful drawing device. I use Procreate a lot, Affinity Photo a lot, and Aseprite and Clip Studio a lot — I've kind of cut ties with Adobe. Affinity is pricier if you haven't already bought it, so you might want to wait until it's on sale, but it's very good and reasonable. Clip Studio moved from a single one-time payment to a subscription model for updates; Aseprite is still a single payment, and there are so many good tutorials for it — highly recommend.

For music software: if you're recording your own music, consider GarageBand for Mac and iPad, or Cakewalk for Windows. If you're looking for pixel/chiptune-style music, both Pico-8 and BeepBox are really good — BeepBox is completely free. I use Cakewalk on Windows and highly recommend it; you can mess around with scales and instruments pretty easily. There are so many free or low-cost tools available. If anyone else has music software they swear by, feel free to drop it in the chat.

For 3D software: the industry standard is Autodesk Maya. What I've mainly used is Blender. I also wanted to mention — even though it's not 3D software — Live2D, which is really cool. It takes a drawing and can make it look semi-real. I think it's great for people starting off in 2D who don't know much about 3D but want to get a character moving. Live2D has a really good, specific tutorial, and it also has an automatic mesh feature — you upload your drawing, click automatic mesh, and it generates a mesh for that drawing.

For those who haven't worked in 3D before: there are really three parts to it. One is the actual sculpting, done in these programs. The second is texturing — you can make your own texture in these programs or upload your own image for a custom texture, using art software to generate it. The third is rigging — building a skeleton, making it move. Some 3D things in your game won't need rigging at all, like a tree or a box with no animation, but if you have a main character who walks around, you'll need to rig it.

Q&A / general advice:

What's your ideal team size? Three to five people. More than that, you need someone to be a project manager. Most people coming to a jam aren't that interested in being a project manager, but if you have that experience, or want to be one, you can join a larger team. A project manager makes sure all the tasks are in order and that when someone finishes a task, it gets passed along properly, since everyone's working on little parts that need to be integrated together — sometimes on Saturday, sometimes on Sunday. If you have five or more people, you tend to need someone helping with that facilitation.

What should you focus on Friday?

Here's the rundown of how the jam works — at least for Let's Games Tokyo. We start letting people in at 5:30, and start the presentation at 6:30. We ask people to be on site by 7 or 7:30, because that's when team creation starts, and missing it can be hard to recover from, especially once people start working on stuff.

Once you have your team, Friday should be about shaping your idea into something fun and doable within the weekend. Global Game Jam has a very limited amount of time, and I think shaping is really important for any game jam — it can actually be harder for a month-long jam, because you have other responsibilities pulling at you and can end up over-scoping. Global Game Jam is laser-focused: 48 hours, and you have to get it done in that time.

Shaping can look like: sketching, whiteboard sketching, digital sketching, getting some initial feedback on the design. You can make an entire game out of paper, or just sketches, and explain it to someone to check if it seems fun — just get a vibe check on whether the idea is worth pursuing.

Then decide the hook and the major gameplay. The hook is what's interesting about your game, what draws players in. For instance, "it's like Metroid but everyone's a dinosaur" — for people who love Metroid, that part hooks them; for people who love dinosaurs but don't know Metroid, that hooks them too. Both angles can grab people.

Then figure out the major gameplay loop. If we're rebuilding something like Metroid-but-dinosaurs, the loop might be: you're in a room, there are enemies, you use your special skill to defeat them, and you get a win condition. Simplify Metroid down to its basic elements — a room, enemies, maybe treasure, a main character who has a skill to progress or defeat enemies.

Also prioritize — figure out what you'll cut if you run out of time. This is an extremely difficult conversation to have on Friday, but I highly suggest you do it, because by Sunday you'll definitely be thinking about what to cut and what's not high priority.

Hot take: don't code on Friday, unless it's to set up version control. Version control lets you roll back changes if something breaks the game — really helpful when multiple people are working on one thing, because otherwise you end up having to manually untangle the mess.

What to focus on Saturday:

Build the core game loop. Remember the game mechanics I mentioned — the game loop builds that out further. For instance: title screen, player opens a chest with a skill in it, goes to a room with enemies, kills the enemies, gets a "good job," and that's the end. That could be a reasonable game loop.

Code out the game mechanics, make basic art assets, story, music. If you're doing 3D assets, Saturday is not the time for rigging or fancy animations — just get the assets into your game, even if they don't animate at all. Building the core game loop is the most important thing to focus on Saturday. Build the entire loop from the start of the game through win and lose conditions — even if you only have one ending, make sure you have at least a complete beginning-to-end.

Sunday:

This is about polishing and integrating assets. I like to say if you're writing for a game jam, you write the beginning and the end of the game, and assume the middle won't be used — but if you have time on Sunday, you build the middle. For instance: "He was a great warrior who had been spurned by his kingdom... [middle missing] ...then he became the great king of the nation." The middle — the journey — is missing, but from a story perspective the gameplay mechanics can fill that in; from a writing perspective, setting up the situation and then resolving it is enough for a core game loop, unless you have multiple levels or stages.

Add any extra characters, add animation if you have time, and hope your game doesn't crash — I can't tell you how many times games crash on Sunday, or Saturday night. If you're lucky it won't happen, but if it does, congratulations, you've gone through a real game jam.

Then focus on uploading and demoing your game. At Let's Games Tokyo, uploads run from 3 to 5 p.m., and demos start around 5 p.m. — usually people need more time, so presentations often start a bit later, around 6. Then we do a little demo party with pizza. It's very neat — I'm really looking forward to Sunday because the energy is super hype, though I'm looking forward to all the days honestly.

So — you've given me very specific advice: don't code on Friday, build the core game loop Saturday, polish on Sunday. What if I just ignore it? Sure, do your thing, you're an adult, everyone at the site is ostensibly 18+, so you do whatever you want, I don't really care — but from ten years of doing Global Game Jam, that's what I'd advise.

Learning to program during the jam:

If you're interested in learning to program during Global Game Jam, that's awesome — you should consider taking the opportunity to learn. Start small. If you have any experience programming with other things, you might look at options for intermediate coders, but if you've never touched code before, I highly recommend low-code or no-code game engine tools.

These are our game jam engine recommendations, put together a while back by myself, Mes, Sharky, and Tipo — we try to update the list every year.

Low-code or no-code options: RPG Maker (though it does require some code for custom stuff), Twine, Bitsy, Decker, Ren'Py, Naninovel, Construct. They're not all strictly low-code or no-code, but you can get started without much programming knowledge. As Mes said, low-code engines can be a great way to prototype ideas too — in fact, Teranova was prototyped in Twine before we built everything else. It's a good way to test things like multiple endings.

For beginner coders: Unity is okay, though it can be overwhelming. Godot is definitely gaining on Unity right now in terms of being easy to get started with. Speaking of Unity — they sent us some goodie bags, including a Game Jam support document, and they're doing a 15% off sale in the Asset Store for Global Game Jam 2025.

I really want to dig into Godot, because out of all the 3D engines I've looked at, it feels like the one that would be easiest for me to sink my teeth into.

For intermediate coders: Pico-8, which I've used for game jams myself — totally learnable within a weekend.

Love Game Jam challenge mode: if you caught Johnny Cash's stream, he mentioned that choosing to learn Unreal Engine at a game jam is, as the British say, "a brave choice." I completely agree that Unreal is very hard to get into — though it's not impossible to be on an Unreal team and learn from others. If everyone on your team is a beginner, though, I'd suggest against jumping straight into Unreal — but again, do what you want, you're an adult.

Phaser and Panda3D are also on the list, though I haven't personally used them that much.

Another one not on the list: Pulp, the engine for the Playdate — if you don't know what a Playdate looks like, it's a really cute little device with its own dev kit. I'm curious about using Pulp; it has a similar engine structure to Bitsy, which I've used pretty extensively.

Non-programming roles:

As I mentioned, here are six other things you can do that aren't programming: game script writer, level designer, environment artist, character artist, sound effects person, or composer. There are many things involved in making video games, and it's very much a group effort — it can be really difficult for one person to do everything, unless you're the kind of person who wants to get into every single thing (which I guess would make me someone good at solo dev). But I actually prefer being in a team — it's much more motivating.

Diversifiers:

Global Game Jam has optional challenges called diversifiers — kind of like challenge mode, or just fun extra goals for your game:

  • Peace Formers — design a web-playable game where players negotiate how resources are shared and divided, keeping the peace among groups fairly and equitably.
  • Nurture Yourself with Nature — design a game that boosts players' mental health and well-being by deepening their connection with nature and easing eco-anxiety.
  • Outplay Hunger — design a game encouraging players to reduce food loss and waste, and support a healthy food diet in their school, community, or country.
  • Control-Alt-Disinfo — create a game that builds critical thinking skills to increase awareness of and skepticism around disinformation.
  • Great Games Come From Everywhere — design a game inspired by unique culture, history, geography, or traditions in your region. I'm sure the Global Game Jam Tokyo crowd will have a lot to say here, given how many international folks we have.
  • Y2K — it's been 25 years since the start of the new millennium; celebrate by implementing early-2000s/late-'90s aesthetics like Y2K futurism, frutiger aero, metal-heart, etc.
  • There's also one about music having a single melody that evolves as the game progresses.

These are all worth checking out — they're actually a great starting point on Friday if you're brainstorming your game idea.

That's it for Global Game Jam for newbies — don't be afraid, get excited. Our jam site is bilingual, so you'll have people speaking a language that might not be your first fluently, which I think is really fun and cool. Get out there and challenge yourself.
